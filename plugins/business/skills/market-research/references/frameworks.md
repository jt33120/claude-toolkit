# Competitive analysis frameworks

## Identifying the competitive set
- **Direct** — same problem, same users, same approach; appear in deals and review-site matchups.
- **Indirect** — same problem, different approach (spreadsheets vs a dedicated tool); include "non-consumption"
  (doing nothing, or a manual process).
- **Adjacent** — do not compete today but could (similar tech/customer base, or a platform that could add this
  as a feature).
- **Substitutes** — hiring a person, a general-purpose tool, outsourcing entirely.

## Feature comparison
Group features into buyer-facing capability areas (not your internal architecture). Rate each competitor:
- **Strong** — market-leading, deep, well-executed.
- **Adequate** — functional, not differentiated.
- **Weak** — exists but limited.
- **Absent** — does not have it.

Rate from real product experience, customer feedback and reviews, not marketing claims. Update regularly —
comparisons go stale fast.

## Positioning statement template
> For [target customer] who [need/problem], [Product] is a [category] that [key benefit]. Unlike
> [competitor/alternative], [Product] [key differentiator].

Reverse-engineer this from the homepage headline, app-store description, and (for public companies) earnings
call language.

### Message architecture (per competitor)
1. Category claim (what do they say they are)
2. Differentiator within that category
3. Value proposition (outcome promised)
4. Proof points (logos, metrics, case studies)

### Positioning map
Plot competitors on two axes that matter for this market (price vs capability, ease of use vs power, SMB vs
enterprise, point solution vs platform). Identify the underserved quadrant.

## Win/loss analysis
Sources: CRM notes (fast, biased), post-decision customer interviews (best, least biased), churn surveys.
Ask wins: what alternatives were evaluated, why us, what almost lost the deal. Ask losses: what did they choose
and why, where did we fall short, would they reconsider. Segment by deal type; distinguish product reasons
(features, quality) from non-product reasons (price, brand, relationship, timing, switching cost).

## Market trend framework
For each trend: what's changing, why now (tech/regulation/behavior/economics), who's affected, timeline (now /
1-2y / 3-5y), implication for us, competitor response. Separate signal (behavioral data, investment, regulatory
action, real customer demand) from noise (media hype, conference buzz with no traction). Response options:
lead, fast-follow, monitor, or explicitly ignore (and say why).
